Abstract

Biomusics is a personal research project at the intersection of biophysics, musicology and sound art. This mission statement describes where the project comes from, the four approaches through which I personally engage with the open theme of Biomusics, and how the corresponding blog is meant to work.

1 Introduction: Where I come from

Biomusics has its roots in my student years. I studied physics and musicology side by side and hold degrees in both. Early in these studies, more than ten years ago, I encountered Hermann von Helmholtz’s Die Lehre von den Tonempfindungen als physiologische Grundlage für die Theorie der Musik [1, 2], and it has stayed with me ever since. Helmholtz was a physicist, a physiologist and a deeply musical thinker, and in this book he attempted something that still seems radical: to found music theory on the physics of sound and on the biology of hearing at once. This motivated me to generalize my original motivation to study musicology, namely music composition, to composition of sounds from a researcher’s perspective.

The two studies then developed in parallel, each in its own direction. In musicology they led ever closer to Helmholtz and sound art. My bachelor’s thesis examined how his theory of tone sensations shaped compositional techniques of the twentieth century, from Schoenberg and Ives to Grisey [3, 4, 5, 6, 7], an arc that runs from the biophysics of hearing straight into compositional practice. In physics they led towards information theory and the physics of complex biosystems, the field of my PhD [8]. Biomusics is where I continue the research begun in both studies, and where the two lines meet.

2 Four approaches

“Biomusics” is a neologism, mostly derived from “biomusic” a broad field with many ways into it. In this section I want to approach this project from four perspectives. The four approaches below, summarised in Figure 1, are not meant as a programme for a field, but as my personal way of approaching it.

2.1 On the sensations of tone

Helmholtz built his theory on the best science of his time: resonance in the cochlea, beating partials, the roughness of dissonance. Some of it has held up remarkably well. Some has been refined or overturned, for instance by the discovery of the active mechanics of the inner ear [9], by research on the neural coding of pitch and timbre, and by evidence that the preference for consonance varies across cultures [10]. The first approach is to revisit the Tonempfindungen piece by piece: what still holds, what needs revision, and what Helmholtz could not yet ask.

2.2 Listening as inference

Helmholtz also introduced the notion of unconscious inference [11]: perception as a conclusion drawn from incomplete sensory evidence. Bayesian and predictive accounts of perception descend directly from this idea [12, 13], and it lies at the core of my work in information theory. Music offers an exceptionally rich testing ground. Expectation, surprise, ambiguity and resolution [14] can be formulated as inference under uncertainty, made by an auditory system that continuously infers what it hears and what comes next [15, 16]. The second approach is to develop music listening as inference, both conceptually and quantitatively.

2.3 Investigating the connection between biology and music

In Biomusics, “bio” deliberately covers several things at once: music as a biological phenomenon, biological systems heard through a musical lens, and perception, learning and evolution as processes that shape and are shaped by sound. Two directions of investigation are central:

  • From biology to sound. Biological processes unfold in rhythms, structures and dynamics that can be made audible. Sonification [17] serves both as an analytical tool, exposing patterns that escape visual inspection, and as material in its own right. Additionally, biological insights can inspire sound works that exploit specific biological effects, such as combination tones or the otoacoustic emissions of the inner ear [9], which some composers have turned into musical material [18].

  • From sound to biology. Here the goal is not sound but understanding: how biological systems perceive acoustic and musical structure. Sound experiments are part of the method, not the result.

2.4 Sonic research art

Biomusics treats sound art as a form of research: the investigation of sound with scientific methods. Typical questions are: How does a sound act on the listener? How can it be described? Which parameters underlie it, and what changes when they are varied? This leads naturally to the idea of meta-music and sound spaces. Instead of fixing a single sound, one describes a whole family of sounds or possible pieces, so that every concrete sound or piece is just one realisation of a more abstract model [19, 20].

The name Biomusics is a deliberate plural. It refers not to one music but to musics: the composition of whole spaces of possible pieces, a meta-music in which any concrete piece emerges as a random sample drawn from a more complex model. The suffix -ics echoes this plural, being itself a plural form that, as in physics [21], the discipline I come from, has come to denote sciences and fields of study, and so signals the scientific character of the approach.

3 Conclusion: How Biomusics works

Biomusics is a work-in-progress blog. It presents current projects at a stage where they can still change, so that colleagues can engage with them early: question assumptions, point to related work, propose collaborations. Once a project matures, the finished article may be published in a peer-reviewed journal and linked from its entry on biomusics.com, in the spirit of a preprint server. The platform is open to contributions by others working at these intersections, whatever their home discipline: physics, biology, musicology, music theory, acoustics, composition or sound art.

Helmholtz’s book opened a field more than 160 years ago. Biomusics is an invitation to keep working on it.
